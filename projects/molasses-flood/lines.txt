In 1919, a wave of molasses as tall as a house chased people through Boston at thirty-five miles an hour.
Boston's North End. A steel tank fifty feet tall, holding two point three million gallons of the stuff, waiting to become alcohol.
The man in charge wasn't an engineer. He tested it with barely any water.
It leaked so much, neighbours filled buckets at the seams.
The company's fix? Paint the tank brown.
Then, in mid-January, the city warmed from two degrees to forty-one.
Just after noon, the rivets popped like gunfire... and the whole tank split open.
Luckily, molasses is famously slow. Unluckily, nobody told this molasses.
It bent the elevated railway, shoved a firehouse off its foundation, and flooded the streets three feet deep.
Twenty-one people died.
Why so deadly? Take honey from the fridge: it barely moves. Warm it, and it pours.
The molasses poured out warm and fast, then the January air thickened it like glue around everyone it caught.
Rescuers could barely wade in.
The company blamed anarchists. The court blamed the tank.
And from then on, big structures in Boston needed an engineer's signature, and the maths to prove it.
The tank's base now sits under a Little League field.
And on hot summer days, locals swore the North End still smelled sweet... for decades.
