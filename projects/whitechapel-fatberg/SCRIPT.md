# The 250-Metre Monster Hiding Under London — SCRIPT (draft v1, awaiting approval)

## Facts (with sources)
- Found Sept 2017 during a routine Thames Water inspection of a Victorian sewer under Whitechapel Road, east London; it threatened to cause flooding. — London Museum, londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/whitechapel-fatberg
- 250 m long (same as Tower Bridge, ~4 football pitches), ~130 tonnes (= 11 double-decker buses). — London Museum; Guinness World Records (world's largest fatberg)
- Sewer 1.2 m tall x 0.7 m wide, totally blocked per CCTV; 3.5 m below ground. — The Construction Index, 14 Sep 2017
- Made of congealed fat, oil, wet wipes, nappies; wet wipes formed its main structure. — London Museum; Construction Index
- Eight-strong crew, high-powered jet hoses (plus shovels, spades, pickaxes), 20-30 t removed per day. — WaterWorld 12 Sep 2017; AP via The Columbian 2018
- Removal took ~9 weeks; cost ~£220,000. — Statista; Thames Water's Alex Saunders via GWR
- Thames Water compares breaking fatbergs to breaking concrete. — National Geographic
- Chemistry: fat reacts with calcium in wastewater (saponification) into hard chalky soap; Cranfield's Raffaella Villa compared it to the scum ring round a bathtub after a long soak. — Atlas Obscura "Meet the Fatbergs"; The Conversation
- Museum of London "Fatberg!" display opened 9 Feb 2018; sample hatched flies (likely coffin/phorid flies), sweated, changed colour, grew toxic aspergillus mould (yellow pustules); FatCam livestream. — London Museum; Smithsonian; SCMP
- Most of it converted to biodiesel (Argent Energy, Ellesmere Port), ~10,000 L, which helps run London buses. — Museum of London; AP/Columbian; The Counter
- England's ban on selling/supplying wet wipes containing plastic signed into law 18 Nov 2025, in force 19 May 2027. — gov.uk guidance "Plastic wet wipes ban"
- 2019 commemorative manhole cover at Court Street / Whitechapel Road. — Archiwik

## Narration (~178 words; ~48 s brisk AI read, ~65 s natural read before pause tightening)
A monster as long as Tower Bridge was hiding under a London street.
September 2017. Whitechapel. Workers sent a camera into a Victorian sewer.
They saw fat. Then more fat. Then wet wipes, nappies and grease, packed wall to wall.
It ran for two hundred and fifty metres. It weighed a hundred and thirty tonnes. As much as eleven double-decker buses.
The sewer was completely blocked, and the street above could flood any day.
Luckily, Thames Water sent eight workers with jet hoses and shovels.
Unluckily, the monster was as hard as concrete. It took them nine weeks.
Why so hard? Because it had turned into soap. Kitchen fat meets the calcium in London's hard water and becomes the same chalky scum that rings your bath. Wet wipes don't break up, so they became its skeleton.
Now England is banning wet wipes made with plastic.
And the monster? One chunk went to the Museum of London, where it hatched flies and grew toxic mould.
The rest was turned into biodiesel.
So the monster that weighed as much as eleven buses now helps to run them.
