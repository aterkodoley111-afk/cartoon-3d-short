A monster as long as Tower Bridge was hiding under a London street.
September 2017. Whitechapel. Workers sent a camera into a Victorian sewer.
They saw fat. Then more fat. Then wet wipes, nappies and grease, packed wall to wall.
It ran for two hundred and fifty metres. It weighed a hundred and thirty tonnes. As much as eleven double-decker buses.
The sewer was completely blocked, and the street above could flood any day.
Luckily, Thames Water sent eight workers with jet hoses and shovels.
Unluckily, the monster was as hard as concrete. It took them nine weeks.
Why so hard? Because it had turned into soap. Kitchen fat meets the calcium in London's hard water and becomes the same chalky scum that rings your bath. Wet wipes don't break up, so they became its skeleton.
Now England is banning wet wipes made with plastic.
And the monster? One chunk went to the Museum of London, where it hatched flies and grew toxic mould.
The rest was turned into biodiesel.
So the monster that weighed as much as eleven buses now helps to run them.
