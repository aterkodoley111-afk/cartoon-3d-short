On Christmas Eve 1953, a volcano destroyed a railway bridge without even erupting. New Zealand. The night express to Auckland carried two hundred and eighty-five people home for Christmas. The sky was clear. It hadn't rained all day. But high on Mount Ruapehu, a crater lake had been filling for eight years behind a wall of loose volcanic ash. That evening, the wall gave way. Two million cubic metres of water raced down the river, swallowing mud, boulders and ice. It hit the Tangiwai bridge and ripped out a pier. Ten minutes later, the express arrived. A postal clerk, Cyril Ellis, ran down the tracks waving his torch. The driver braked. Too late. The engine and five carriages plunged into the river. One hundred and fifty-one people died. Luckily, the sixth carriage stopped right on the edge. Unluckily, Ellis had just climbed inside to warn everyone. Then it fell too. He smashed a window with his torch and helped save twenty-one people. Picture a bathtub with a plug made of sand. It holds for years, then the water wears through, and the whole bath empties at once. No eruption needed. So New Zealand put alarms on the river. In 2007, the plug broke again, with an even bigger wave. This time the alarms rang, the trains stopped, and no one was hurt. Nobody needed a torch.
