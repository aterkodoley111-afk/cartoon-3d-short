# The Volcano That Didn't Erupt (Tangiwai, Christmas Eve 1953) — draft v1

## Facts (with sources)
- 24 Dec 1953, 22:21: the Whangaehu River rail bridge at Tangiwai collapsed under Wellington-Auckland express No. 626; 285 aboard, 151 died; NZ's worst rail disaster. Ruapehu was NOT erupting. (Wikipedia "Tangiwai disaster"; Te Ara)
- Cause: the tephra (volcanic ash) dam left by the 1945 eruption, holding back the refilled Crater Lake, collapsed; ~2 million m3 of water picked up rocks, silt, trees and ice. (Te Ara railway accidents p3; Christchurch City Libraries)
- Timeline: 20:02 crater rim gives way; ~22:10-22:15 lahar (6 m wave) hits bridge, washes away pier 4; 22:20 train passes Tangiwai station. (NZHistory node 5232)
- Clear night, no rain all day. (Christchurch City Libraries; Brett 2023)
- Locomotive: oil-burning steam loco KA 949, eleven carriages; driver Charles Parker. (Brett 2023; Wikipedia)
- Cyril Ellis, 27, Taihape postal clerk, saw the road bridge submerged, ran along the track waving a torch; brakes applied ~300 m before bridge. (Te Ara historic volcanic activity p5; NZTerritory; Brett 2023)
- Engine + five carriages fell; sixth carriage (car Z, first class) teetered; Ellis and guard William Inglis climbed in to warn passengers; coupling snapped, it fell. Ellis broke a window with his torch; with John Holman helped save 21 lives. George Medal. (NZHistory node 796; Army Museum citation)
- Legacy: lahar warning devices on the river/bridges; ERLAWS from 2002. 18 Mar 2007 lahar (as big or bigger than 1953) — alarms worked, trains and cars stopped, no casualties. (Wikipedia ERLAWS; USGS HVO 2013)
- Queen Elizabeth II arrived in NZ 23 Dec 1953 and ended her Christmas broadcast from Auckland with sympathy for Tangiwai. (Wikipedia royal tour; gg.govt.nz) — not in script (length)

## Script (paste-ready, ~215 words, ~58 s at a brisk 3.7 w/s read)
On Christmas Eve 1953, a volcano destroyed a railway bridge without even erupting. New Zealand. The night express to Auckland carried two hundred and eighty-five people home for Christmas. The sky was clear. It hadn't rained all day. But high on Mount Ruapehu, a crater lake had been filling for eight years behind a wall of loose volcanic ash. That evening, the wall gave way. Two million cubic metres of water raced down the river, swallowing mud, boulders and ice. It hit the Tangiwai bridge and ripped out a pier. Ten minutes later, the express arrived. A postal clerk, Cyril Ellis, ran down the tracks waving his torch. The driver braked. Too late. The engine and five carriages plunged into the river. One hundred and fifty-one people died. Luckily, the sixth carriage stopped right on the edge. Unluckily, Ellis had just climbed inside to warn everyone. Then it fell too. He smashed a window with his torch and helped save twenty-one people. Picture a bathtub with a plug made of sand. It holds for years, then the water wears through, and the whole bath empties at once. No eruption needed. So New Zealand put alarms on the river. In 2007, the plug broke again, with an even bigger wave. This time the alarms rang, the trains stopped, and no one was hurt. Nobody needed a torch.
